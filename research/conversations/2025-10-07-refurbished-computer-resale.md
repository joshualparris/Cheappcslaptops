# Refurbished Computer Resale Conversation — 7 Oct 2025

This note preserves the substantive content of Josh's ChatGPT conversation about getting into refurbished-computer resale, including the inventory examples and estimates discussed at the time.

## Starting point

Josh asked how to get into buying cheap computers, refurbishing them, and reselling them for more. Constraints given:
- Operate from home.
- No startup cash.
- Only a small screwdriver set initially.
- Open to local or online sales.
- Available time was roughly 8–9 pm, three nights per week.

The suggested low-risk model was to start extremely small: roughly one flip per fortnight, using one evening to scout, one to clean/refurbish/test, and one to photograph/list/sell. The emphasis was on free or very cheap working machines and simple jobs such as cleaning, SSD/RAM changes and OS reinstalls rather than deep board repair.

## Four-week pilot discussed

Week 1: set up a basic inventory/profit tracker, prepare installation media/tools, learn local pricing, and look for free/cheap machines.

Week 2: acquire a first candidate, test it, clean/reset it, then photograph and list it.

Week 3: repeat the workflow and record actual costs, time and sale result.

Week 4: review which listings and machine types attracted interest, improve the workflow, and only reinvest proven profit.

## Parts resale estimates discussed in 2025

These were rough asking/value ranges discussed in the chat, not current September 2026 valuations:
- 2.5-inch SATA HDDs, roughly 160–500 GB: $5–$15 each if healthy.
- Older SSDs: roughly $10–$25 depending on capacity/health.
- DDR2 1–2 GB SO-DIMMs: roughly $3–$10 each.
- DDR3 2–4 GB SO-DIMMs: roughly $5–$15 each.
- Genuine tested OEM chargers: roughly $10–$25.
- Older laptop LCD panels: roughly $10–$25, dependent heavily on exact panel model and condition.
- Keyboards: roughly $5–$15.
- Fans/heatsinks: roughly $3–$10.
- Old batteries: usually little/no value unless verified healthy.
- Optical drives: roughly $3–$8.

The chat estimated a fully parted Core 2 Duo-era laptop might contain roughly $25–$50 of individually saleable parts, while Pentium M/Celeron M machines might be closer to $10–$20. Those figures did not account for listing time, postage, platform fees, returns, or how slowly obscure parts can sell.

## Important correction / lesson

Some earlier advice in the conversation was too categorical in calling old hardware 'e-waste' and some resale estimates were optimistic. For the ongoing Cheappcslaptops project, exact model/part numbers, tested condition, recent sold listings, postage/fees, and time-to-sell should be checked before deciding to dismantle or sell a machine whole. Older machines can also have collector/retro value that is not captured by ordinary office-PC pricing.

## Practical strategy captured from the conversation

Given the limited weekly time, the useful business test is not 'how much can every old computer theoretically yield in parts?' but 'which machines create worthwhile profit per hour?' Prioritise easy-to-test complete machines and common, searchable parts. Avoid spending hours stripping and individually listing $3–$10 components unless there is proven demand or they can be bundled.
