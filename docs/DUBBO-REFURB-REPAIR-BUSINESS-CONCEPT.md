# Dubbo Refurb & Repair — Business Concept

**Status:** concept / pilot proposal  
**Updated:** 1 October 2026  
**Market:** Dubbo, NSW  
**Core idea:** acquire low-cost dead, untested or incomplete computers, repair only the economically sensible ones, refurbish them to a transparent tested standard, and resell them locally — while using genuinely difficult failures for training, parts or donor stock rather than forcing every device into a commercial repair.

> This document is a business concept, not a claim that any named local business currently holds a particular licence or has agreed to participate.

---

## 1. The opportunity

There are really **two businesses** hiding inside the same skill set:

### A. Refurb-and-resell
Buy cheap or untested laptops/desktops, repair and clean them, then resell them as ready-to-use local machines.

### B. Customer-owned repair
Repair computers and phones that already belong to customers.

The second model can be attractive because it avoids tying up cash in stock and reduces the risk of buying an unknown dead device. The first model can create larger per-unit gains when sourcing is excellent.

The strongest overall model may eventually combine both:

> **repair revenue + selective refurbishment + donor/parts salvage + advanced repair capability over time**

---

## 2. What should actually be bought for resale

The profit is made **at purchase**, not by installing Windows.

### Preferred laptop stock

Prioritise:

- Dell Latitude
- HP EliteBook / ProBook
- Lenovo ThinkPad
- Intel 8th generation or newer where practical
- comparable officially supported Ryzen systems
- 8 GB RAM minimum target
- 256 GB SSD target
- usable battery
- obtainable charger
- screens, hinges and chassis that are economical to restore

### Preferred desktop stock

Prioritise:

- Dell OptiPlex
- HP EliteDesk / ProDesk
- Lenovo ThinkCentre
- 8th-gen Intel or newer where practical
- common SFF/mini-tower formats with plentiful parts
- systems that can be made Windows 11-ready without unusual work

### Good faults

These are commercially attractive when the acquisition price is right:

- no SSD / dead SSD
- insufficient RAM
- missing charger or standard PSU
- broken Windows installation
- dirty/overheating cooling system
- degraded thermal paste
- simple keyboard replacement
- simple battery replacement
- damaged SATA drive
- straightforward DC jack / cable issue
- CMOS / BIOS / configuration faults
- known replaceable screen fault where parts are cheap
- machines sold as "untested" that prove healthy

### High-risk faults

Treat these as training, donor-stock or specialist jobs unless the purchase price leaves a very large margin:

- unknown no-power motherboard fault
- liquid damage
- CPU/PCH/GPU failure
- severe PCB corrosion
- burnt board layers
- cracked PCB
- BGA / reball work
- badly damaged USB-C power circuitry
- multiple previous repair attempts
- missing proprietary high-cost assemblies

The YouTube-style "miracle resurrection" is brilliant for learning. It is not automatically good commercial work.

## 3. Working unit economics

These are **decision targets**, not guaranteed sale prices.

### Laptop target

| Item | Working target |
|---|---:|
| Dead/untested acquisition | $10–$50 |
| Better complete repairable business laptop | up to ~$80–$120 all-in if resale supports it |
| Parts + consumables | keep as low as practical |
| Typical local resale target | ~$150–$250 for mainstream refurb stock |
| Better 10th-gen+ units | potentially higher |
| Minimum visible gross room before buying | ~$80–$100 |
| Preferred gross room | ~$100+ |

A machine that looks like a $200 sale but already costs $120 before parts is usually a poor flip.

### Example

**8th-gen i5 Latitude / EliteBook / ThinkPad**

- purchase: $45
- 256 GB SSD: $25
- charger: $20
- cleaning/consumables: $5
- total cash cost: $95
- achieved sale: $220
- gross room before labour/overheads/returns: **$125**

That has enough room to absorb negotiation or a surprise.

If the same machine costs $110 before repair, the opportunity is far less attractive.

## 4. Refurb standard

The differentiator should not be "I installed an SSD."

Every resale machine should aim for a repeatable standard:

- exterior cleaned properly
- internal dust removed where practical
- thermals checked
- SSD health recorded
- battery health recorded
- RAM tested
- keyboard tested
- trackpad/mouse input tested
- screen checked for damage/dead pixels
- Wi-Fi tested
- Bluetooth tested where present
- webcam tested
- speakers/headphone output tested
- USB ports tested
- charging tested
- sleep/wake tested
- Windows Update completed
- activation status checked
- BIOS/firmware updated where appropriate
- charger included and identified
- faults/cosmetic defects disclosed
- serial number and test result recorded

A local buyer should be able to see **what was tested and what they are buying**.

Positioning:

> **Affordable + local + transparent + tested + approachable**

## 5. Phones

Phones can fit the same business, but should initially be treated as a separate risk tier.

### Lower-risk phone work

- battery
- screen
- charging-port assembly where modular
- camera module
- speaker / microphone module
- cleaning
- software restore
- data transfer
- known-good housing/assembly replacement

### Advanced work

- logic-board shorts
- water-damaged boards
- microsoldering
- damaged pads/traces
- IC replacement
- BGA work
- data-recovery board repair

Advanced phone-board work could become a differentiator, but requires microscope work, hot-air/rework equipment, practice boards and a much higher skill threshold.

## 6. The advanced-repair skill path

A long-term advantage would be moving beyond swap-and-replace repair into **component-level diagnosis**.

Relevant learning areas:

1. schematics and boardviews
2. bench-power-supply current analysis
3. resistance/diode-mode diagnostics
4. power rails
5. MOSFETs and charging circuits
6. short finding / thermal methods
7. microscope soldering
8. pad and trace reconstruction
9. BIOS/SPI work
10. USB-C power-delivery faults
11. BGA rework only after strong fundamentals

Reference channels are collected here:

- [Dead → Alive: Elite Tech Repair YouTube Watchlist](REPAIR-YOUTUBE-WATCHLIST.md)

For DadLAN-style laptops, the most transferable channels are Electronics Repair School, NorthridgeFix, Mend It Mark and Louis Rossmann. Northwest Repair is a later-stage GPU/BGA reference.

## 7. Operating structures

### Option A — independent business

The business buys the used devices, holds them, repairs them and resells them.

This offers maximum control, but also means carrying the relevant licensing/compliance, stock risk, warranty/consumer obligations, tools, premises and operating costs.

### Option B — operate inside an established licensed business

Potentially much more attractive for an early pilot.

The **business itself** would acquire the stock, record it through its systems, hold it as required, authorise the repair, own the resale transaction and handle the relevant compliance.

A technician could then perform the refurbishment as part of that business.

This is not the same as personally buying stock and informally "using" somebody else's licence.

Potential local/employer partners can be investigated privately, but their licence status, approved premises, insurance, approval and operating model must be verified before relying on them.

### Option C — repair-only first

Start with customer-owned computers/phones and charge for:

- diagnosis
- labour
- upgrades
- cleaning
- OS recovery
- parts

This avoids speculative stock purchasing and can fund tools and training.

Selective refurb stock can be added later.

## 8. NSW second-hand compliance

Electronic goods are prescribed second-hand goods in NSW.

Current NSW guidance says a second-hand dealer licence is generally required to operate a business buying and selling prescribed second-hand goods. The current **2026–27 new 1-year licence fee is $692**.

There are also requirements around transaction records, supplier identification/ownership information, reporting, business/storage premises and retention of goods. NSW material refers to a **14-day retention period** for second-hand goods unless an exemption applies.

Because exemptions and business structures can change how the rules apply, the actual operating entity should verify its obligations with NSW Fair Trading before launch.

## 9. Pilot proposal

Do **not** start by buying a pile of dead laptops.

Run a controlled three-machine pilot.

### Machine 1 — easy win
A business laptop with a known simple problem:
- missing/dead SSD
- missing RAM
- missing charger
- OS fault

Goal: prove the refurb/test/listing process.

### Machine 2 — moderate repair
A machine with one hardware fault:
- keyboard
- battery
- fan/thermal issue
- screen
- DC jack

Goal: measure real labour and parts margin.

### Machine 3 — dead/untested
A genuinely dead or unknown machine bought very cheaply.

Goal: establish:
- diagnostic time
- repair probability
- donor/parts fallback value
- point at which the repair should be abandoned

Record for every machine:

- acquisition cost
- acquisition source
- diagnosis
- parts
- time spent
- consumables
- final test
- listing price
- enquiries
- offers
- actual sale price
- time-to-sale
- return/warranty issues
- final profit
- effective $/hour

## 10. Commercial stop rules

The business needs rules that protect it from interesting-but-unprofitable repairs.

Suggested starting rules:

- do not buy a normal flip without **~$80–$100 visible gross room**
- set a diagnostic time limit before reassessing
- do not order expensive parts until the rest of the machine is tested
- salvage donor value when the repair case collapses
- do not hide defects
- do not treat labour as free when evaluating the model
- separate **learning projects** from **commercial repairs**
- older unsupported machines must be extremely cheap or have a specific buyer/use case
- avoid inventory that will sit for months simply because the repair was satisfying

## 11. Why Dubbo may suit this model

The existing research indicates a genuine local refurbished-computer category.

A local operator can offer things online sellers cannot easily provide:

- local pickup
- buyer can test before paying
- human explanation
- setup help
- fast replacement machine after a failure
- transparent battery/SSD health
- known local contact
- affordable price below new retail
- repair support after purchase

That suggests the opportunity is not just **"cheap computers"**.

It is:

> **trusted, tested, affordable computers with someone local who actually understands how to repair them.**

---

## 12. What success would look like

Do not judge the idea by revenue alone.

Track:

- gross profit per device
- profit after valuing labour
- average repair time
- repair success rate
- donor/salvage recovery rate
- average days-to-sale
- buyer enquiry rate
- returns/rework
- stock cash tied up
- repeat/referral customers
- repair jobs generated by refurb customers

A good first threshold would be proving that **three to ten machines can be processed consistently without hidden labour destroying the margin**.

Only then scale sourcing.

## 14. Recommended next move

**Run one pilot through an existing compliant business environment if available and authorised.**

Before touching stock:

1. confirm the operating business/legal entity;
2. confirm whether it actually holds the required current second-hand dealer licence;
3. confirm the relevant premises/storage arrangement;
4. confirm who buys and owns the stock;
5. confirm how acquisition/holding/reporting records are handled;
6. agree how parts, labour, sale revenue and profit are accounted for;
7. choose one low-risk business laptop;
8. record the entire unit economics from acquisition to sale.

That single machine will answer more than another month of theoretical research.
